# Next short: "The Man Whose Rare Blood Saved Over Two Million Babies" (research, 2026-11-18)
Status: researched, not scripted yet. Subject: **James Harrison** (1936-2025), Australia's "Man with the Golden Arm".

## Verified facts (with sources)
| Fact | Source(s) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| At 14 (1951) he had major chest surgery and received **13 units** of blood; he pledged to become a donor. Some outlets say "13 litres"; Lifeblood says 13 units. | Lifeblood media release (11 May 2018); Wikipedia | High (use "13 units" / "lots of blood") |
| He started donating at 18 (1954), the minimum age, and kept going until **81** (last donation **11 May 2018**). | Lifeblood 2018; MJA 2018 (Thyer et al.); AP 2025 | High |
| **1,173 donations** (Lifeblood also gives 1,162 right arm + 10 left = 1,172; some obits say "more than 1,100"). | MJA 2018; Lifeblood 2018; AABB 2025 | High (say "over 1,100" or "1,173") |
| His plasma held unusually strong anti-D antibodies; scientists **suspect** the teenage transfusions triggered them (not proven). | Lifeblood 2018 ("scientists suspect"); Lifeblood story page | Medium: phrase as "doctors think" |
| He was the first donor of Australia's anti-D program, which started in **August 1967**; the first dose went to a pregnant woman at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, Sydney, in 1967. | MJA 2018; Lifeblood 2018 | High |
| The disease: when an RhD-negative mother carries an RhD-positive baby, her antibodies can attack the baby's red blood cells (haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn): miscarriage, stillbirth, brain damage, fatal anaemia. Anti-D works like a passive vaccine that stops the mother's immune system from learning to attack. | Lifeblood 2018; MJA 2018 | High |
| Scale before anti-D: about **250** HDFN stillbirths and newborn deaths estimated for Australia in 1967 (MJA); Lifeblood's release says "thousands" a year. In Victoria deaths fell from 1.1 to 0.01 per 1,000 births. | MJA 2018 (more rigorous); Lifeblood 2018 | Use MJA numbers or say "hundreds of babies a year" |
| **2.4 million babies**: Lifeblood's estimate from national birth data since 1964 × share of mothers given anti-D × HDN death risk. It is an estimate of babies helped, not a count. | Lifeblood 2018; AABB 2025 | High that the claim is Lifeblood's; say "an estimated" |
| About 17 % of pregnant women in Australia need anti-D, **including his own daughter**. | Lifeblood 2018 | High |
| He never watched the needle: "I look at the nurses, the ceiling, the spots on the wall, anything but the needle." (Often retold as a fear of needles.) | Lifeblood 2018 | High (quote) |
| Retired in 2018: past the donor age limit, Lifeblood chose to protect his health. | Lifeblood 2018 | High |
| Guinness World Records recognised him in 2005 for the most blood plasma donated; an American donor overtook the record in 2022. | AP obituary 2025 (via several outlets) | Medium-high |
| Died **17 February 2025**, aged 88, in a nursing home on the NSW Central Coast. | AP / Lifeblood / AABB 2025 | High |
| Today anti-D in Australia relies on fewer than 200 donors (MJA 2018: ~130). WEHI and Lifeblood are developing a lab-made (recombinant) anti-D from donors' antibody cells. | Lifeblood research update; WEHI partnering docs; MJA 2018 | High that the work exists; no product yet |

**Unverified / avoid:** an OAM medal in 1999 (not confirmed in this search), "he saved 2.4 million lives" (it is an estimate of babies *helped*), "his plasma is in every dose ever made worldwide" (Australia only), exact "13 litres".

## Why it can work as a short
- One clear human hero, a promise made at 14, a number that keeps growing (1,173), and a twist that his own daughter needed the medicine his blood made.
- Visual: a "golden arm", a counter ticking up, a mother and baby protected by a shield, antibodies as little soldiers attacking red blood cells.

## Beat outline (~60 s)
1. Hook: 14-year-old James on a hospital bed, blood bags lined up (13 units). → "He promised to give it back."
2. Age 18: first donation; he looks at the ceiling, never the needle.
3. The problem: RhD-negative mother, RhD-positive baby, antibodies attacking the baby's red cells (cartoon cells).
4. The discovery: his blood is full of the exact antibody that stops it. Scientists made a medicine: anti-D (1967).
5. Escalation: every two weeks, for decades; counter ticks 1 → 1,173; golden arm.
6. Twist: his own daughter needed anti-D.
7. Deadpan double: "Luckily, he never missed. Unluckily... he never watched the needle." (or similar real-fact joke)
8. Consequence: an estimated 2.4 million babies; retired at 81 in 2018; died 2025 at 88.
9. Callback: scientists are now trying to copy his blood in the lab, so the promise keeps going.
