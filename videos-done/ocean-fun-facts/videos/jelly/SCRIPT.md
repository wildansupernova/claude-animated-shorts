## Facts (sources)
- *Turritopsis dohrnii*, the "immortal jellyfish", is about 4.5 mm across, smaller than a pinky nail. (AMNH, "The
  Immortal Jellyfish"; National Geographic 2009)
- Normal hydrozoan life: a polyp attached to the bottom buds off free-swimming medusae (jellyfish), which grow,
  reproduce and die. *T. dohrnii* medusae can instead undergo reverse development in response to physical damage,
  adverse environmental conditions or aging; starving newly released medusae is used in the lab to trigger it. The
  medusa shrinks, loses its swimming ability, settles on the bottom and forms a cyst ("blob"); within about 24-36 h
  the cyst grows a stolon (root-like hydrorhiza) and new polyps arise, which then bud new medusae.
  (Matsumoto, Piraino & Miglietta 2019, *G3* 9(12):4127-4138, doi:10.1534/g3.119.400487)
- First experimental documentation: Piraino, Boero, Aeschbach & Schmid 1996, "Reversing the life cycle: medusae
  transforming into polyps and cell transdifferentiation in *Turritopsis nutricula*", *Biological Bulletin* 190:302-312
  (the animals are now assigned to *T. dohrnii*). Isolated exumbrellar epidermis (outer "skin") could still turn into
  a polyp: differentiated cells switch to other cell types (transdifferentiation). (Piraino et al. 1996; PeerJ 2018
  review; Matsumoto et al. 2019)
- Shin Kubota (Seto Marine Biological Laboratory, Kyoto University) recorded one specimen of the immortal jellyfish
  genus (*Turritopsis* sp., collected in Okinawa) rejuvenating from medusa to polyp 10 times between 18 May 2009 and
  31 March 2011 (about 22 months), the most then recorded. (Kubota 2011, "Repeating rejuvenation in *Turritopsis*, an
  immortal hydrozoan", *Biogeography* 13:101-103)
- It is not truly immortal: it can still be eaten or killed by disease. (AMNH; Pascual-Torner interview coverage 2022)
- Worldwide spread: medusae from Japan, Panama, Florida, Spain and Italy are almost genetically identical, a silent,
  human-mediated invasion, most likely via ships' ballast water. (Miglietta & Lessios 2009, "A silent invasion",
  *Biological Invasions* 11:825-834; National Geographic, Jan 2009)
- 2022: University of Oviedo (Spain) sequenced *T. dohrnii* and its non-rejuvenating relative *T. rubra* and found
  variants and expansions (extra copies) of genes for DNA replication and repair, telomere maintenance, redox and stem
  cells. (Pascual-Torner, Carrero, ... Quesada 2022, "Comparative genomics of mortal and immortal cnidarians unveils
  novel keys behind rejuvenation", *PNAS* 119(36):e2118763119)

## Script (voice: Kokoro `am_puck`, see voice/lines.txt)
1. This jellyfish doesn't die of old age... it just starts over.
2. Meet the immortal jellyfish. It's smaller than your pinky nail.
3. Normally, a jellyfish's life only goes one way.
4. A tiny polyp, stuck to a rock, buds off a baby jellyfish. It grows up... and then, that's it.
5. But starve this one, hurt it, or just let it get old...
6. and it shrinks. Its tentacles pull in. It sinks to the bottom... and turns into a blob.
7. In a couple of days, the blob grows roots... and becomes a baby polyp again.
8. Then it buds brand new jellyfish. Life cycle: reset.
9. It's like a butterfly crawling back into its chrysalis... and coming out a caterpillar.
10. The trick? Its cells can switch jobs. A skin cell can turn into a completely different kind of cell.
11. In Japan, scientist Shin Kubota watched one jellyfish and its clones do this ten times... in under two years.
12. Luckily, it never dies of old age.
13. Unluckily... it's still a snack.
14. And it's spreading. Scientists think it hitched rides around the world in ships' ballast water.
15. In 2022, researchers in Spain read its DNA, and found extra copies of genes that repair it.
16. So somewhere out there right now... a jellyfish is starting its eleventh childhood.

- No factual errors found. Changes made for precision: butterflies form a **chrysalis** (moths spin cocoons); Kubota
  followed one founder jellyfish **and its clones** (each reset used a new medusa budded from the previous polyp
  colony, 18 May 2009 to 31 Mar 2011); the transdifferentiation card says **NEW JOB** instead of NERVE (the papers show
  outer-skin cells becoming polyp tissue, not specifically nerve cells).
- Kept as is: "doesn't die of old age" / "never dies of old age". The evidence is from the lab (Piraino et al. 1996:
  every mature medusa tested reverted), which is how the species is described everywhere ("biologically immortal").
- Visual fixes: microscope zoom no longer crops the side cells; the lab push-in keeps the scientist's face in frame;
  the budding baby jellyfish is bigger; the Panama → Japan route crosses the Pacific instead of Africa and Asia.
