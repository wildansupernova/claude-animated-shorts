## Facts (sources)
- At 14 (1951) James Harrison had major chest surgery and "depended on the blood of strangers to save his life";
  he received 13 units of blood and pledged to donate as soon as he was old enough; four years later (at 18) he kept
  the promise "despite an aversion to needles". (Lifeblood, "James Harrison's story"; Lifeblood media release 11 May 2018)
- "Over a decade later" it was discovered that his blood contained the antibody needed to make anti-D; his body
  produces a lot of it (scientists suspect the transfusions, unproven, not claimed in the video). (Lifeblood story;
  Lifeblood 2018)
- Haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn: an RhD-negative mother carrying an RhD-positive baby can become
  sensitised and her antibodies attack the baby's red blood cells, causing miscarriages, stillbirths, brain damage or
  fatal anaemia. Anti-D works like a passive vaccine that stops the mother's own immunisation. (Lifeblood 2018;
  Thyer et al., *Med J Aust* 2018;209(8):336-339)
- Australia's anti-D program began in 1967; the first dose went to a pregnant woman at Royal Prince Alfred Hospital,
  Sydney. Harrison donated from the program's start. (MJA 2018; Lifeblood 2018)
- 1,173 donations over more than 60 years, from 18 until his final donation at 81 on 11 May 2018, after passing the
  donor age limit. Plasma can be given as often as every two weeks ("every few weeks" in the video). (MJA 2018;
  Lifeblood 2018; Lifeblood story)
- About 17 % of pregnant women in Australia need anti-D, including his own daughter. (Lifeblood 2018)
- "The man with the golden arm". (Lifeblood story; Lifeblood 2018)
- He never watched the needle: "I look at the nurses, the ceiling, the spots on the wall, anything but the needle."
  (Lifeblood 2018)
- Lifeblood estimates his donations helped protect 2.4 million babies (national birth data since 1964 × share of
  mothers given anti-D × HDN death risk); more than 3 million doses containing his plasma issued since 1967.
  (Lifeblood 2018; Lifeblood story; AABB 2025)
- Died 17 February 2025, aged 88. (AABB "In Memoriam: James Harrison", 4 Mar 2025; AP obituary 2025)
- Lifeblood and WEHI (Prof. Ian Wicks) are developing a lab-grown anti-D from anti-D donors' antibody cells; they
  believe they have a combination that works comparably to the donor product and are considering clinical trials.
  (Lifeblood research update "Understanding the anti-D immune response")

2. James Harrison, from Australia, had major chest surgery in 1951... and needed thirteen units of blood.
3. So he made a promise: as soon as he was old enough, he'd give blood too.
4. At eighteen, he kept it. Even though he hated needles.
5. Years later, doctors found his plasma was packed with a powerful antibody called anti-D.
6. If a mother's blood is Rh-negative, and her baby's is Rh-positive,
7. her body can learn to treat the baby's blood like an invader... and in her next pregnancy, attack it.
8. Babies were born sick, brain-damaged... or not at all.
9. His antibodies became a medicine: a shot that stops the mother's body from learning to attack.
10. In 1967, the first mother got it.
11. From age eighteen, he kept coming back every few weeks... for over sixty years.
12. One of the mothers who needed that shot... was his own daughter.
13. Australians called him the Man with the Golden Arm.
14. Luckily, that arm kept going until he was eighty-one.
15. Unluckily... he never once watched the needle.
16. His donations are estimated to have helped protect 2.4 million babies.
17. He died in 2025, at eighty-eight.
18. Now scientists are trying to make anti-D in a lab... so his promise never has to end.
