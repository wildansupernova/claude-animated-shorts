# "Bread and Bombs from Thin Air" (Fritz Haber) — original script v1 (2026-11-14)
Format: ~65 s vertical short, ~3.7 words/s, one steady male narrator, no captions. Our own characters and story.

## Facts (verified, with sources)
- ~1900 fertilizer nitrogen came from guano and Chilean saltpeter, both running out; Crookes' 1898 "wheat problem" speech. (Nature 061169a0; Erisman et al. 2008, Nature Geoscience)
- Wars over guano/nitrates: Chincha Islands War 1864-66 (Spain seized Peru's guano islands); War of the Pacific 1879-84 (nitrates). (Wikipedia; Britannica)
- Air is ~78 % nitrogen; plants can't break the N≡N triple bond. (Wikipedia, Atmosphere of Earth)
- 2 July 1909, Karlsruhe: Haber's tabletop reactor, ~175-200 atm, ~500-600 °C, osmium catalyst, dripped liquid ammonia (~100 g/h) for ~5 h for BASF. (Sheppard 2017, PMC5554788; Smil, Nature 1999)
- Carl Bosch scaled it up; Oppau plant from 9 Sept 1913. (Smil 1999)
- "The lives of around half of humanity are made possible by Haber-Bosch nitrogen" (48 %, Erisman 2008). ~half of the nitrogen in the human body passed through Haber-Bosch, an estimate (Ritter, C&EN 2008 via Wikipedia "Haber process").
- WWI: British blockade cut Germany off from Chilean nitrate; Haber-Bosch ammonia → nitric acid → explosives kept Germany fighting. (FHI biography of Haber; Erisman 2008)
- 22 April 1915, Ypres: ~5,730 cylinders, ~168 t chlorine; Haber supervised; ~1,000 dead. (Wikipedia, Second Battle of Ypres; FHI)
- Clara Immerwahr, first woman doctorate at Breslau (1900), died by suicide 2 May 1915; Haber left for the Eastern Front next day. (Friedrich & Hoffmann 2016, PMC4825402) — no method shown or said.
- On the Allies' list of alleged war criminals; grew a beard, fled to Switzerland; charges dropped; 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry announced 1919. (FHI biography)
- Scientists at his institute developed Zyklon A; Zyklon B (Heerdt, 1922) became the Nazi gas-chamber poison; members of his extended family died in the camps. (Wikipedia, Zyklon B)
- Often called "the father of chemical warfare".

## Script
One man saved billions of lives and helped kill millions, and both times, his secret weapon was the air.
It's 1909, and the world is running out of fertilizer.
The best stuff is bird poop, and it's so valuable that countries literally go to war over it.
The crazy part? Every breath you take is seventy-eight percent nitrogen, but plants can't crack it open.
So a stubborn German chemist, Fritz Haber, squeezes air two hundred times harder than normal, cooks it at five hundred degrees, and his little steel machine starts dripping ammonia.
Fertilizer, out of thin air.
Today, that trick feeds about half the people on Earth.
But the same nitrogen that grows wheat also makes explosives, and when the First World War cuts Germany off from imported nitrates, his process keeps its guns firing.
Then he goes further. Near Ypres, he personally oversees a hundred and sixty-eight tons of chlorine gas, rolling into enemy trenches.
Days later, his wife Clara, a brilliant chemist herself, takes her own life. The next morning, he leaves for the front.
After the war, he's wanted as a war criminal, so he grows a beard and hides in Switzerland.
A few months later, he wins the Nobel Prize.
Later, his institute's pest-killing research leads to a gas called Zyklon. A later version becomes the Nazis' gas chamber poison, and his own relatives die in the camps.
So, by some estimates, half the nitrogen in your body was pulled from the air by the father of chemical warfare.
