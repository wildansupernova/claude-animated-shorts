# "The Woman Who Never Stopped" (Henrietta Lacks) — original script v1 (2026-11-15)
Format: ~62 s vertical short, ~3.7 words/s, one steady narrator, burned-in captions. Our own characters and story; respectful tone.

## Facts (verified, with sources)
- Born Loretta Pleasant, 1 Aug 1920, Roanoke VA; raised by her grandfather on a tobacco farm in Clover VA; moved to Turner Station near Baltimore (1941); five children. (Britannica; ASU Embryo Project)
- Jan–Feb 1951: treated at Johns Hopkins, "one of only a few" hospitals treating poor Black patients (segregated ward); cervical cancer; on 8 Feb 1951, during her first radium treatment, a tumour sample was taken without her knowledge or consent (legal and routine then). Died 4 Oct 1951, aged 31. (Johns Hopkins Medicine; WHO 2021; Skloot)
- In George Gey's lab, where other samples died, her cells "doubled every 20 to 24 hours"; Mary Kubicek grew and labelled them HeLa. (Johns Hopkins Medicine)
- Immortality: HPV-18 integrated into the cancer cells' DNA, switching off the limits that stop normal cells dividing. (Nature 2013, nature12064)
- Used to test Salk's polio vaccine (Tuskegee shipped HeLa from 1953 for the 1954 field trial); cancer, HIV, HPV vaccine, gene mapping; first human cells cloned (1955); in 1960 among the first human cells sent into orbit. (Scientific American; WHO; Smithsonian; Skloot)
- More than 110,000 publications used HeLa (1953–2018). (NIH OSP)
- Family learned her cells were alive more than twenty years after her death (1973 per Skloot); no compensation for ~70 years. (Smithsonian)
- 1 Aug 2023: the Lacks family settled with Thermo Fisher Scientific (terms confidential). (Smithsonian; CBS)
- Epitaph (headstone 2010): "Her immortal cells will continue to help mankind forever." (NPR)

## Script
This woman died in 1951, but her cells are still alive today, and they've even been to space.
It's 1951. Henrietta Lacks is a thirty-year-old mother of five who grew up farming tobacco in Virginia.
She goes to Johns Hopkins, one of the few hospitals that will treat Black patients.
Doctors find cervical cancer, and during treatment, they take a piece of her tumor without asking her.
It lands in George Gey's lab, where human cells keep dying within days.
Hers don't. They double about once a day, and never stop.
You see, normal cells have a built-in countdown, but a virus in her tumor had switched hers off.
Henrietta dies that October, at thirty-one. Her cells keep going.
Scientists name them HeLa, mail them around the world, and use them to test the polio vaccine, fight cancer, map our genes, and even send human cells into orbit.
They show up in more than a hundred thousand studies.
Luckily, her cells helped protect millions. Unluckily, nobody ever asked her.
Her family only learns her cells are alive more than twenty years later, and for decades, they don't see a cent.
In 2023, they finally settle with a company that sold her cells.
So right now, somewhere in a lab, a piece of Henrietta is still dividing, and this time, the world knows her name.
